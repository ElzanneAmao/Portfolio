Building Think Orion's onboarding system

ClientThink Orion
Year2025
RoleContent Marketing Strategist (ad hoc project taken on alongside core role)

Starting without a process

When I joined Think Orion in January 2025, there was no formalised onboarding process. One of the cofounders outlined what he wanted: brand overview, audience personas, competitor analysis, brand guidelines, channel-specific hooks. From there, I worked out how to structure and format each deliverable.

The first client we ran this process with took about three to four days to get the documents produced, shared, and approved. The next several clients took roughly the same. By the fourth or fifth round, the AI-driven research was getting faster and a repeatable structure had started to take shape.

What the brief was asking for vs. what the team needed

When a new client signed, the partner success managers and media buyers were the ones who had to launch campaigns, often with very little to work from. Clients come to an agency because they need marketing help, which means they arrive with scattered ideas rather than a clear strategic foundation. The team on the receiving end had to piece together messaging, audiences, and positioning from whatever came through in the sales conversation.

The onboarding research changed that. From the first round of deliverables, the partner success managers and media buyers had tangible material to work with: documented personas with specific pain points they could write ads against, competitor positioning mapped out so they could see where to differentiate, and channel-specific hooks for paid, email, and landing pages. They didn't have to guess at messaging or experiment with angles that might be wrong. The research gave them a solid, research-backed foundation to launch from, and it meant the client's first experience of the agency was one where they felt understood.

What the research caught

One particular client's research surfaced a gap that contradicted their core marketing claim. The claim related to a health outcome that peer-reviewed literature suggests can be managed but not cured in the way the client was presenting it. The flag was raised internally within the first week, and the finding became relevant context for how the agency managed the relationship going forward. It's a good example of why thorough research matters early: it gives the team visibility into risks that a sales conversation alone won't surface.

"Diagnostic depth surfaces things the sales conversation can't."

Systemising the process

By the time the agency had built up a body of onboarding documents across multiple clients, one of the cofounders built a custom marketing research platform from scratch, using the outputs from the earlier builds as the structural foundation for how the system organises and generates research. What had taken three to four days now takes about three hours.

What changes at scale

Running the systemised version across roughly twenty programmes at a prominent Middle Eastern university's online faculty over the past months, I've been able to see what holds up at volume. The research structure is preserved. The depth of per-client familiarity changes: when I ran the earlier version on those first clients, I knew them well enough to brief the partner success managers from memory. At twenty programmes, I know the deliverables but I don't carry the same intuitive understanding of each client's specific position. That's one of the things being refined as the platform develops, and it's where the human operator's role remains essential to the quality of the output.

Outcomes

The time reduction from three to four days to roughly three hours per client is the clearest operational result. Beyond that, client satisfaction with the research documents has been consistently high. Clients have responded to the onboarding materials with the kind of recognition that sets the tone for the working relationship: a sense that the agency understands their brand and their audience from the start.

Where this goes

The platform is still being developed, and the question I'm working through is a practical one: what kind of knowledge should a content operations system encode, what kind does it lose at speed, and where does a human operator need to stay in the loop for the work to hold its depth? One of the more interesting parts of my current role is being inside that question while the system is still being built.
